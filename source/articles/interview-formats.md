# Phone screens, panels and case interviews

**Covers:** Phone screen, Panel interview, Case interview

## One job, several kinds of interview {#overview}
You might expect a job interview to be one conversation. Often it is not. Companies add steps to save time or to test different skills, and each step asks something different of you. So the first question to ask is, "What is this step for?" A step that checks basic fit needs a tidy summary of your background. A step with many people needs a way to share your eye contact. A step with a problem needs a calm method. Let's look at three common ones.

## Phone screen {#phone-screen}
A phone screen is a short first call, often 15 to 30 minutes, usually with a recruiter. Its purpose is to check the basics: your experience, your pay expectations, your availability and your interest.

Treat it as a real interview. Find a quiet place, keep your resume in front of you and have a one-minute summary ready. Smile while you talk, because a smile changes your voice, even when nobody can see it. Before you hang up, ask what the next step is.

## Panel interview {#panel-interview}
In a panel interview, several interviewers ask you questions in one meeting. They may come from different teams, so each cares about something different: the manager about results, a peer about teamwork, someone from human resources about the company rules.

When a question arrives, look at the person who asked it as you start your answer. Then let your eyes move around the group as you continue. Write down every name when you are introduced, and use them when you refer back: "As Priya asked earlier..." At the end, thank everyone.

## Case interview {#case-interview}
In a case interview you get a business problem and work through it aloud. A typical prompt is "A coffee shop's sales dropped by 10 percent. What would you do?" Nobody expects a perfect answer. The interviewer is listening to how you think.

A plain method works well. Repeat the problem to check you understood it. Ask clarifying questions. Split the problem into parts, such as customers, prices and costs. Test each part with a quick calculation. Finish with a recommendation and one risk.

## A worked example {#example}
Let's try a short case together. A coffee shop sells 500 cups a day at 4 dollars each, and sales fell by 10 percent.

1. Today's sales: 500 cups times 4 dollars is 2,000 dollars a day.
2. A 10 percent fall is 200 dollars a day, which is about 50 cups.
3. Ask: did the number of customers drop, or did each customer buy less?
4. If a new shop opened nearby, test a loyalty card. If prices rose, test a smaller size at the old price.
5. Recommend the cheapest test first and say what number would prove it worked.

The numbers are small on purpose. What matters is the order of the steps: clarify, split, calculate, recommend.

## A common mistake {#mistake}
A common slip is staying silent while you think. In all three formats the interviewer cannot read your mind. On the phone and in a case, silence feels much longer than it is. Say "Let me think for a moment", or narrate what you are considering.

## Key points {#key-points}
- A phone screen checks basic fit, so be ready with a clear summary and a quiet space.
- In a panel interview, answer to everyone and use their names.
- A case interview rewards a clear method more than a perfect answer.
- Think aloud, so the interviewer can follow you.
