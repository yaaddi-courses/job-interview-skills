# Presenting yourself: pitch, listening and practice

**Covers:** Elevator pitch, Active listening, Mock interview

## The three habits behind a good first impression {#overview}
An interview is partly about what you say and partly about how it feels to talk with you. Three habits cover most of it. You can introduce yourself in a few plain sentences. You can listen well enough to answer the question that was asked. And you can practice until none of it feels new. Each one can be learned, and each one makes the others easier.

## Elevator pitch {#elevator-pitch}
Imagine you step into a lift with someone important and have one ride to introduce yourself. What you would say is your elevator pitch. About 30 to 45 seconds is the right length.

A useful shape has three parts: who you are now, one or two things you do well, and what you want next. Keep to facts that help with this particular job. You will use the pitch when an interviewer says "tell me about yourself", and also at job fairs and networking events.

## Active listening {#active-listening}
Active listening means giving the speaker your full attention and letting them see it. In an interview it does two jobs. It lets you answer the real question. And it shows the interviewer how you would listen to a customer or a manager.

Some habits help. Look at the speaker, nod lightly, and do not plan your answer while they are still talking. When the question is long, pause for a second before you reply. If a question is unclear, say it back in your own words: "So you are asking how I handle disagreements with a manager?"

## Mock interview {#mock-interview}
A mock interview is a practice run with a friend, a coach or a camera. After one, the real day feels familiar. A recording adds something more: it lets you see habits you cannot notice while speaking, such as "um", fast speech or looking away.

Ask your partner to write questions from the real job posting, and to interrupt with follow-ups, because real interviewers do. Afterwards, ask for two things that went well and one thing to change. Then repeat your weakest answer straight away, while the feedback is fresh.

## A worked example {#example}
Let's build a pitch for someone applying to a bakery manager role, using the three parts.

1. Who I am: "I am a baker with four years of experience, the last two as shift lead."
2. What I do well: "I train new staff quickly and cut waste by tracking each day's sales."
3. What I want: "I want to run a team of my own, which is why your manager role interests me."

Read aloud, this takes about 20 seconds, so there is room for one more sentence with a number: "Last year my shift cut waste by 15 percent."

Now test it in a mock interview. Ask your partner to check three things. Could they repeat your pitch back? Did you keep eye contact? Did you pause before answering?

## A common mistake {#mistake}
A common slip is memorizing the pitch word for word. It then sounds like a recording, and one interruption can make you lose your place. Learn the three parts and the one number, and say it in your own words each time.

## Key points {#key-points}
- An elevator pitch is a 30 to 45 second summary: who you are, what you do well, what you want.
- Active listening helps you answer the real question and shows how you work with people.
- A mock interview, ideally recorded, shows the habits you cannot see yourself.
- Learn the shape of your answers, not the exact words.
