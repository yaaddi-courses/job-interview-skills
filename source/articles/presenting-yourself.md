# Presenting yourself: pitch, listening and practice

**Covers:** Elevator pitch, Active listening, Mock interview

## The three habits behind a good first impression {#overview}
An interview is partly about what you say and partly about how it feels to talk with you. Three habits cover most of it. You can introduce yourself in a few plain sentences. You can listen well enough to answer the question that was asked. And you can practice until none of it feels new. Each one is easy to learn, and each improves the others.

## Elevator pitch {#elevator-pitch}
An elevator pitch is your introduction at its shortest. The name comes from the idea that you could say it during a short ride with someone important. About 30 to 45 seconds is right.

A simple shape has three parts: who you are now, one or two things you do well, and what you want next. Keep it to facts that help this particular job. You will use it for "tell me about yourself" in interviews, and also at job fairs and networking events.

## Active listening {#active-listening}
Active listening means paying full attention and showing it. In an interview it does two jobs. First, it lets you answer the real question. Second, it shows the interviewer how you would listen to a customer or a manager.

Practical habits: look at the speaker, nod lightly, and do not plan your answer while they are still talking. When the question is long, pause for a second before you respond. If a question is unclear, repeat it in your own words: "So you are asking how I handle disagreements with a manager?"

## Mock interview {#mock-interview}
A mock interview is a practice run with a friend, coach or camera. Doing it once makes the real day feel familiar, and doing it with a recording lets you see habits you cannot notice while speaking, such as "um", fast speech or looking away.

Ask your partner to use the real job posting to write questions, and to interrupt with follow-ups, because that is what interviewers do. Afterwards, ask for two things that went well and one thing to change. Then repeat the weakest answer straight away.

## A worked example {#example}
A pitch for someone applying to a bakery manager role, built from the three parts:

1. Who I am: "I am a baker with four years of experience, the last two as shift lead."
2. What I do well: "I train new staff quickly and cut waste by tracking each day's sales."
3. What I want: "I want to run a team of my own, which is why your manager role interests me."

Read aloud, this takes about 20 seconds, so there is room for one more sentence with a number: "Last year my shift cut waste by 15 percent."

Now test it. In a mock interview, ask your partner to check three things: could they repeat your pitch back, did you keep eye contact, and did you pause before answering?

## A common mistake {#mistake}
Memorizing the pitch word for word. It sounds like a recording, and a single interruption can make you lose your place. Learn the three parts and the one number, then say it in your own words every time.

## Key points {#key-points}
- An elevator pitch is a 30 to 45 second summary: who you are, what you do well, what you want.
- Active listening helps you answer the real question and shows how you work with people.
- A mock interview, ideally recorded, shows the habits you cannot see yourself.
- Learn the shape of your answers, not the exact words.
