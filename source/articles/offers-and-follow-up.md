# After the interview: thanks, pay and references

**Covers:** Thank-you note, Salary range, Total compensation, Counteroffer, Reference

## The interview is not the end {#overview}
Some of the most useful work happens after you leave the room. A short thank-you can tip a close decision. Knowing the pay range helps you respond to an offer with confidence. And prepared references stop a good process from stalling at the last step. This article follows the order in which these things usually happen.

## Thank-you note {#thank-you-note}
A thank-you note is a brief email sent within 24 hours of the interview. It does three things: thanks the person for their time, mentions one specific point from the conversation, and says you are still interested.

Keep it to four or five sentences. Mentioning something specific proves you were listening: "I enjoyed hearing how your team handles weekend shifts." If you met several people, send each one a slightly different note, not the same text. Check the spelling of every name before you send.

## Salary range {#salary-range}
A salary range is the lowest and highest pay the company will offer for the role. Many postings state it, and some places must do so by law. If it is not stated, look at similar roles on job sites and ask people in the field.

You may be asked about your expectations early. A safe reply is to give a range of your own, based on the market, with your target near the middle: "For this kind of role in this city, I am looking at 52,000 to 60,000, depending on the full package."

## Total compensation {#total-compensation}
Total compensation is everything you earn from a job, not only the salary. It includes bonuses, health insurance, retirement contributions, paid time off, learning budgets and other perks.

Comparing offers by salary alone can mislead you. A job paying 55,000 with full health cover and a bonus can be worth more than one paying 58,000 with neither. Ask for the details in writing and add them up.

## Counteroffer {#counteroffer}
A counteroffer is your reply to a job offer in which you ask for different pay or terms. It is normal and companies often expect it, especially when the first number is below the middle of their range.

Be polite and specific. Thank them, say you are excited, and give a reason and a number: "Based on my experience and the market, I was hoping for 60,000. Is there room to get closer to that?" If pay is fixed, you can ask for something else, such as an extra week of leave.

## Reference {#reference}
A reference is a person who can describe your work and character to a possible employer, such as a former manager or a long-term colleague. Ask first, and ask early. Tell each person about the job so that they can give a relevant answer, and send them your updated resume.

## A worked example {#example}
Two offers compared by total compensation:

| | Offer A | Offer B |
|---|---|---|
| Salary | 58,000 | 55,000 |
| Bonus | 0 | 3,000 |
| Health cover paid by company | No (about 4,800 a year to buy) | Yes |
| Paid leave | 10 days | 20 days |

Offer B adds the bonus and saves you about 4,800 in health costs. In money, B is worth about 55,000 + 3,000 + 4,800 = 62,800 compared with 58,000 for A, before counting the extra ten days of leave.

## A common mistake {#mistake}
Accepting the first number straight away because asking feels rude. Companies plan for negotiation. A polite counteroffer rarely loses an offer, and an unspoken wish never gets met.

## Key points {#key-points}
- Send a short, specific thank-you note within 24 hours.
- Know the salary range for the role before you are asked about pay.
- Compare offers by total compensation, not only the salary.
- A polite counteroffer is normal. Give a reason and a number.
- Ask your references early and tell them about the job.
