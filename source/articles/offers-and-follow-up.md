# After the interview: thanks, pay and references

**Covers:** Thank-you note, Salary range, Total compensation, Counteroffer, Reference

## The interview is not the end {#overview}
When you walk out of the room, it can feel as if the work is over. It is not. A short thank-you can tip a close decision. Knowing the pay range helps you answer an offer with confidence. And prepared references stop a good process from stalling at the last step. We will follow these in the order they usually happen.

## Thank-you note {#thank-you-note}
Within 24 hours of the interview, send a brief email. It does three things: it thanks the person for their time, mentions one specific point from the conversation, and says you are still interested.

Keep it to four or five sentences. The specific point shows you were listening: "I enjoyed hearing how your team handles weekend shifts." If you met several people, send each one a slightly different note, not the same text. Check the spelling of every name before you press send.

## Salary range {#salary-range}
Before anyone talks about your pay, it helps to know the band the company has in mind: the lowest and highest amounts it will offer for the role. Many postings state it, and in some places the law requires it. If it is missing, look at similar roles on job sites and ask people in the field.

Sometimes you are asked about your expectations early. A safe reply gives a range of your own, based on the market, with your target near the middle: "For this kind of role in this city, I am looking at 52,000 to 60,000, depending on the full package."

## Total compensation {#total-compensation}
Salary is only one part of what a job pays you. Total compensation counts everything: bonuses, health insurance, retirement contributions, paid time off, learning budgets and other perks.

This matters because comparing offers by salary alone can mislead you. A job paying 55,000 with full health cover and a bonus can be worth more than one paying 58,000 with neither. Ask for the details in writing and add them up.

## Counteroffer {#counteroffer}
When an offer arrives, you are allowed to answer it. If you reply by asking for different pay or terms, that reply is a counteroffer. It is normal, and companies often expect it, especially when their first number is below the middle of their range.

Be polite and specific. Thank them, say you are excited, and give a reason and a number: "Based on my experience and the market, I was hoping for 60,000. Is there room to get closer to that?" If the pay is fixed, you can ask for something else, such as an extra week of leave.

## Reference {#reference}
A reference is someone who can describe your work and character to a possible employer, such as a former manager or a long-term colleague. Ask first, and ask early. Tell each person about the job, so that they can give a relevant answer, and send them your updated resume.

## A worked example {#example}
Let's compare two offers by total compensation.

| | Offer A | Offer B |
|---|---|---|
| Salary | 58,000 | 55,000 |
| Bonus | 0 | 3,000 |
| Health cover paid by company | No (about 4,800 a year to buy) | Yes |
| Paid leave | 10 days | 20 days |

Offer B pays a 3,000 bonus, and it saves you about 4,800 in health costs. So in money, B is worth about 55,000 + 3,000 + 4,800 = 62,800, compared with 58,000 for A. That is before we count the extra ten days of leave. The lower salary turns out to be the better offer.

## A common mistake {#mistake}
A common slip is accepting the first number right away because asking feels rude. Companies plan for negotiation. A polite counteroffer rarely loses an offer, while a wish you never mention cannot be met.

## Key points {#key-points}
- Send a short, specific thank-you note within 24 hours.
- Know the salary range for the role before you are asked about pay.
- Compare offers by total compensation, not only the salary.
- A polite counteroffer is normal. Give a reason and a number.
- Ask your references early and tell them about the job.
