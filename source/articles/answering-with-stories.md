# Answering with stories

**Covers:** STAR method, STAR, Behavioral question, Situational question

## Why stories work {#overview}
Interviewers cannot watch you do the job, so they ask about it instead. A real story is the best evidence they can get. It is concrete, it is hard to fake, and it is easy to remember. The risk is rambling: a story told without a plan can take three minutes and never reach the point.

This article shows the two common question types and a four-part pattern that keeps every answer short and complete.

## Behavioral question {#behavioral-question}
A behavioral question asks about something you really did. The usual starts are "Tell me about a time when..." or "Give me an example of...". The idea behind them is that past behavior is the best guide to future behavior.

Typical subjects are conflict, deadlines, mistakes, leading others and learning something new. Prepare five or six stories ahead of time. One good story can often be reused for several questions by stressing a different part.

## Situational question {#situational-question}
A situational question describes an imaginary problem and asks what you would do. For example: "A teammate keeps missing deadlines. What would you do?" It is used most when you have little experience, because there may be no past example to share.

Answer it with a short plan, not a guess. Say what you would find out first, what you would do, and how you would know it worked. If you have faced something similar, add one sentence about it: "I did this once, and it worked because..."

## STAR method {#star-method}
STAR is a pattern with four parts, and the initials give it its name:

1. **Situation:** the background, in one or two sentences.
2. **Task:** your responsibility in that situation.
3. **Action:** what you did, step by step. This is the longest part.
4. **Result:** what happened, with a number if you have one, and what you learned.

Spend about a fifth of your time on the first two parts and most of the rest on action and result. The listener should finish knowing what you personally did, not what the team did.

## STAR {#star}
Interviewers often say "give me a STAR answer" or write STAR in their scoring sheet. When you hear the word, it is the same pattern described above, so the structure is expected and welcome. Using it makes their notes easier to fill in, which helps you.

## A worked example {#example}
Question: "Tell me about a time you solved a problem under pressure."

| Part | What the candidate says |
|---|---|
| Situation | "Last December, our shop's card machine broke on the busiest Saturday." |
| Task | "I was the only supervisor on duty, so it was my job to keep sales going." |
| Action | "I put up a sign, moved customers to cash and a phone payment app, and asked a colleague to call the provider." |
| Result | "We lost about 20 minutes of sales instead of the afternoon, and I wrote a one-page backup plan for the next time." |

The whole answer takes about 40 seconds. It names a number, a personal role and something learned.

## A common mistake {#mistake}
Telling only the situation. Candidates love the background, but the interviewer is waiting to hear your action and its result. If you notice you have spoken for half a minute without saying "I did", stop and move to the action.

## Key points {#key-points}
- A behavioral question asks what you did; a situational question asks what you would do.
- STAR means Situation, Task, Action, Result, and it keeps an answer short and complete.
- Spend most of the time on your own actions and the result.
- Prepare a handful of stories before the interview and reuse them.
