# Answering with stories

**Covers:** STAR method, STAR, Behavioral question, Situational question

## Why stories work {#overview}
Think about it from the interviewer's side. They cannot watch you do the job, so they ask you to describe it. A real story is the best evidence they can get, because it is concrete, hard to fake and easy to remember. The risk is on your side: a story told without a plan can run for three minutes and never reach the point.

In this article we will look at the two kinds of question you will be asked, and then at a four-part pattern that keeps every answer short and complete.

## Behavioral question {#behavioral-question}
A behavioral question asks you to describe something you really did. You will hear "Tell me about a time when..." or "Give me an example of...". The thinking behind it is that what you did before is the best guide to what you will do next.

The usual subjects are conflict, deadlines, mistakes, leading others and learning something new. So prepare five or six stories ahead of time. One good story can often answer several questions if you stress a different part each time.

## Situational question {#situational-question}
A situational question describes an imaginary problem and asks what you would do. For example: "A teammate keeps missing deadlines. What would you do?" Interviewers use it most when you have little experience, because there may be no past example to share.

Don't guess. Give a short plan instead. Say what you would find out first, what you would do, and how you would know it worked. If you have faced something similar, add one sentence about it: "I did this once, and it worked because..."

## STAR method {#star-method}
Here is the pattern that keeps a story under control. It has four parts, and their first letters give it its name:

1. **Situation:** the background, in one or two sentences.
2. **Task:** your responsibility in that situation.
3. **Action:** what you did, step by step. This is the longest part.
4. **Result:** what happened, with a number if you have one, and what you learned.

Spend about a fifth of your time on the first two parts and most of the rest on action and result. When the listener finishes, they should know what you personally did, and not only what the team did.

## STAR {#star}
You may hear an interviewer say "give me a STAR answer", or see the word on their scoring sheet. It is the same pattern you met a moment ago, so they are asking for the structure above. Using it makes their notes easier to fill in, and that helps you.

## A worked example {#example}
Let's answer the question "Tell me about a time you solved a problem under pressure", one part at a time.

| Part | What the candidate says |
|---|---|
| Situation | "Last December, our shop's card machine broke on the busiest Saturday." |
| Task | "I was the only supervisor on duty, so it was my job to keep sales going." |
| Action | "I put up a sign, moved customers to cash and a phone payment app, and asked a colleague to call the provider." |
| Result | "We lost about 20 minutes of sales instead of the afternoon, and I wrote a one-page backup plan for the next time." |

Spoken aloud, the whole answer takes about 40 seconds. It has a number, a personal role and something learned, which is what the interviewer was listening for.

## A common mistake {#mistake}
A common slip is telling only the situation. The background is comfortable to talk about, but the interviewer is waiting for your action and its result. If you notice you have spoken for half a minute without saying "I did", stop and move on to the action.

## Key points {#key-points}
- A behavioral question asks what you did. A situational question asks what you would do.
- STAR means Situation, Task, Action, Result, and it keeps an answer short and complete.
- Spend most of the time on your own actions and the result.
- Prepare a handful of stories before the interview and reuse them.
